Dialysis Catheter Care: How to Prevent Bloodstream Infections

A dialysis catheter has the highest infection risk of any dialysis access. This guide covers the US CDC steps that dialysis units use to stop blood infections, the care steps for staff, and what patients should do at home.

PBy Pulse Team · Updated November 21, 2026 · 4 min read
Share

To prevent dialysis catheter infections, staff should scrub the catheter hub with an antiseptic every time it is opened, clean the exit site with alcohol-based chlorhexidine, apply antibiotic or povidone-iodine ointment at dressing changes, and wear a mask during connection. The US CDC says units that followed these steps cut dialysis bloodstream infections by about half.

Why Catheters Get Infected

A dialysis catheter is a tube that sits in a large vein. One end stays outside the body. Each time it is opened or the dressing is changed, germs can get into the blood. That is why a fistula is the best long-term access, and why catheter care must be strict. See our AV fistula vs catheter guide.

The CDC Core Interventions

InterventionWhat it means in practice
TrackingCount blood infections each month and share the rates with staff
Hand hygiene auditsWatch hand hygiene every month and give feedback
Access care auditsWatch how staff connect, disconnect and dress catheters every 3 months
Staff trainingTrain on access care and aseptic technique; check skills at hiring and every 6 to 12 months
Patient educationTeach every patient about access care, hand hygiene and signs of infection
Fewer cathetersMove patients from catheters to a fistula or graft
Chlorhexidine skin prepUse alcohol-based chlorhexidine (above 0.5%) for insertion and dressing changes
Scrub the hubScrub the catheter hub with antiseptic every time it is opened or disconnected
Exit-site ointmentApply antibiotic or povidone-iodine ointment at dressing changes

If a patient cannot use chlorhexidine, the CDC says povidone-iodine (best with alcohol) or 70% alcohol can be used.

Does It Work?

Yes. In a 2013 CDC report on 17 dialysis units, these steps cut blood infections by about half.

Catheter Care Steps for Staff

  1. Check the catheter and the skin around it before every connection.
  2. Wash hands, wear a mask and use aseptic technique.
  3. Remove the cap and scrub the hub with antiseptic. Let it dry before connecting.
  4. Clean the exit site with chlorhexidine and alcohol at each dressing change.
  5. Apply ointment or use a chlorhexidine sponge dressing.
  6. Change the dressing at least once a week, or sooner if it is wet, loose or dirty.

These steps follow the CDC core interventions and a US National Kidney Foundation care sheet.

What Patients Should Do at Home

  • Keep the dressing clean and dry. Do not swim, and avoid showering unless your unit says how to protect the catheter.
  • Never let anyone open the catheter outside the dialysis unit.
  • Wash your hands before touching the dressing.
  • Call your dialysis unit at once if you have fever, chills, or redness, pain or pus at the exit site.

  • Scrub the hub every time the catheter is opened or disconnected.

Dialysis Catheter Care: FAQs

How do you prevent dialysis catheter infections?

Scrub the hub with antiseptic each time it is opened, clean the exit site with alcohol-based chlorhexidine, apply ointment at dressing changes, wear a mask and use aseptic technique.

What does scrub the hub mean?

Scrubbing the catheter hub with an antiseptic after the cap is removed and before connecting, every time the catheter is accessed or disconnected.

How often should a dialysis catheter dressing be changed?

At least once a week, or sooner if the dressing is wet, loose or dirty.

Can I shower with a dialysis catheter?

Keep the dressing dry. Avoid swimming, and avoid showering unless your dialysis unit has shown you how to protect the catheter.

What are signs of a catheter infection?

Fever, chills, or redness, pain or pus at the exit site. Call your dialysis unit straight away.
