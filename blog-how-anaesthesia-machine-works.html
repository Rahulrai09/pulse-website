How an Anaesthesia Machine Works: A Simple Guide to the Parts

An anaesthesia machine keeps a patient asleep and breathing during surgery. This guide follows the gas from the wall or cylinder to the patient and back, and explains each part and safety feature in simple words.

PBy Pulse Team · Updated November 22, 2026 · 4 min read
Share

An anaesthesia machine takes oxygen, air and other gases from the hospital pipeline or cylinders, lowers their pressure, and lets the anaesthetist set the flow. A vaporiser adds anaesthetic vapour, a breathing circuit carries it to the patient and removes carbon dioxide, a ventilator can breathe for the patient, and scavenging takes waste gas out of the theatre.

The Four Jobs of the Machine

A StatPearls review lists four main jobs:

  • Give the patient oxygen.
  • Mix the anaesthetic vapour accurately.
  • Help the patient breathe.
  • Keep anaesthetic gas away from theatre staff.

When it has a built-in ventilator and monitors, it is often called a workstation.

Following the Gas

StageWhat happens
1. Gas supplyOxygen, air and nitrous oxide come from the hospital pipeline, or from cylinders on the back of the machine if the pipeline fails
2. Pressure regulatorsCylinder gas is stored at very high pressure (about 2,200 psi for oxygen). A regulator lowers it to about 45 psi, close to pipeline pressure (about 50 psi)
3. FlowmetersThe anaesthetist sets how many litres a minute of each gas flow on
4. VaporiserFresh gas passes through a vaporiser that adds a set amount of anaesthetic vapour
5. Breathing circuitGas goes to the patient through tubes and one-way valves; breathed-out carbon dioxide is removed
6. VentilatorBreathes for the patient when needed, instead of squeezing the bag by hand
7. ScavengingExtra gas is captured and carried out of the theatre

The Vaporiser

Gas drugs used to put people to sleep, such as sevoflurane, are stored as liquids. The vaporiser turns a set amount into vapour and adds it to the gas. Each vaporiser is made for one drug only. A dial sets the dose.

The Breathing Circuit

  • One-way valves keep gas moving in a circle: in through one tube, out through the other.
  • CO2 absorber: a canister of granules that soaks up the carbon dioxide the patient breathes out, so the rest of the gas can be breathed again. The granules change colour as they get used up; StatPearls says to change them when about two-thirds have changed.
  • Reservoir bag: the anaesthetist can squeeze it to breathe for the patient by hand.
  • APL valve: a pressure relief valve. It is left open when the patient breathes on their own and partly closed when breathing for them.

Built-in Safety Features

  • Pin index and diameter index systems: each gas has its own shaped fitting, so a cylinder or pipeline cannot be plugged into the wrong inlet.
  • Hypoxic guard: oxygen and nitrous oxide controls are linked, so the machine will not deliver a mix with less than 21% oxygen.
  • Vaporiser interlock: only one vaporiser can be turned on at a time.
  • Oxygen flush: a button that sends 35 to 70 litres a minute of pure oxygen in an emergency. It skips the vaporiser, so it must be used with care.
  • Alarms and monitors: modern machines watch oxygen, carbon dioxide and pressure and sound an alarm when something is wrong.

How an Anaesthesia Machine Works: Key Facts

  • An anaesthesia machine gives oxygen, mixes anaesthetic vapour, helps breathing and protects staff from waste gas.
  • Cylinder oxygen is about 2,200 psi; a regulator lowers it to about 45 psi, near pipeline pressure.
  • Each vaporiser is made for one anaesthetic drug.
  • A CO2 absorber removes breathed-out carbon dioxide so gas can be breathed again.
  • A hypoxic guard stops mixes with less than 21% oxygen.

How an Anaesthesia Machine Works: FAQs

How does an anaesthesia machine work?

It takes gases from the pipeline or cylinders, lowers their pressure, sets the flow, adds anaesthetic vapour, and delivers it to the patient through a breathing circuit with a ventilator.

What are the main parts of an anaesthesia machine?

Gas supply and regulators, flowmeters, vaporiser, breathing circuit with CO2 absorber and valves, ventilator, monitors and scavenging system.

What is the difference between an anaesthesia machine and a workstation?

A workstation is an anaesthesia machine with a built-in ventilator, monitors and alarms in one unit.

What does the vaporiser do?

It turns a set amount of liquid anaesthetic into vapour and adds it to the fresh gas. Each vaporiser is made for one drug.

What stops the machine giving too little oxygen?

A hypoxic guard links the oxygen and nitrous oxide controls so the mix cannot fall below 21% oxygen, and an oxygen alarm warns staff.
