How a Dialysis Machine Works: A Simple Step-by-Step Guide

Our kidneys clean our blood all day. When they stop working well, a dialysis machine can do that job for them. This guide explains, step by step and in simple words, what happens inside the machine.

How does a dialysis machine work? A pump takes blood out of the body through a needle or tube. The blood goes through a filter called a dialyser. Inside the filter, a special cleaning fluid called dialysate pulls waste and extra salt out of the blood. The machine also takes out extra water. Then the clean blood goes back into the body. A session at a centre usually takes about 4 hours, three times a week.

First, What Do Kidneys Do?

Think of the kidneys as the body's cleaners. Every day they take waste and extra water out of the blood. That waste leaves the body as urine. The kidneys also keep the right amount of salt and minerals in the blood.

When the kidneys fail, waste and water build up in the body. This can make a person very sick. Dialysis does the cleaning job instead. It does not cure kidney failure, but it helps people feel better and live longer.

Step by Step: What Happens During Dialysis

Step 1: Connecting the patient

The nurse puts two needles into a special spot in the arm called a fistula. One needle takes blood out. The other brings clean blood back. Some patients have a soft tube called a catheter instead, usually in the neck or chest.

Step 2: The pump moves the blood

A pump on the machine gently pulls blood through the tubes. Only a small amount of blood is outside the body at any time. The rest of the blood keeps flowing in the body as normal.

Step 3: The filter cleans the blood

The dialyser is full of thousands of very thin, hollow tubes. Blood flows inside these tubes. The cleaning fluid flows outside them, the other way. The tube walls have tiny holes. Waste and extra salt pass through the holes into the cleaning fluid. Blood cells are too big to pass, so they stay in the blood.

Step 4: Taking out extra water

The machine gently pulls extra water out of the blood too. Doctors call this ultrafiltration. The nurse sets how much water to take out, based on how much the patient gained since the last session.

Step 5: Clean blood goes back

The clean blood flows back into the body through the second needle. This loop keeps going for the whole session.

Step 6: The machine watches everything

All the time, the machine checks for air bubbles, blood leaks and pressure that is too high or too low. If something looks wrong, it stops the blood pump and sounds an alarm.

The Science in Simple Words: Diffusion and Ultrafiltration

A dialysis machine cleans blood using two simple ideas:

  • Diffusion (removing waste): waste such as urea and creatinine moves from where there is a lot of it (the blood) to where there is little (the fresh dialysate), through the thin wall of the dialyser. Fresh dialysate keeps flowing in, so there is always room for more waste to move across.
  • Ultrafiltration (removing water): the machine creates a small pressure difference across the wall, which pushes extra water out of the blood. The nurse sets the amount based on how much weight the patient gained since the last session.

Blood and dialysate flow in opposite directions (counter-current flow). This keeps the difference in waste levels high along the whole length of the filter, so more waste is removed in each session.

Why the Water Must Be Very Clean

The cleaning fluid is made by mixing special salts with water. During a session, the blood is separated from this fluid only by a very thin wall. So the water must be extremely clean. Dialysis centres use a water cleaning plant, called an RO plant, to make it safe. International standards such as the ISO 23500 series set how clean dialysis water must be.

Ways to Connect a Patient

  • Fistula: a surgeon joins an artery and a vein in the arm. It is the best long-term choice. It gives strong blood flow and gets infected less often.
  • Graft: a soft man-made tube joins an artery and a vein. It can be used sooner, but it gets infected or blocked more often.
  • Catheter: a soft tube placed in a large vein in the neck, chest or groin. It is often used for a short time until a fistula is ready.

How Long and How Often?

At a dialysis centre, most people have dialysis three times a week. Each session usually lasts about 4 hours. Some people do dialysis at home, with shorter sessions more often. Your kidney doctor decides what is right for you.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does a dialysis machine clean the blood?

A pump sends blood through a filter called a dialyser. A cleaning fluid called dialysate flows on the other side of thin walls inside the filter. Waste and extra salt pass from the blood into the fluid, and the clean blood goes back into the body.

Does dialysis hurt?

Most people feel the needles going in, like a blood test. The cleaning itself does not hurt. Some people feel tired, cold or have cramps during or after a session. Tell the nurse if you feel unwell.

How long does one dialysis session take?

At a dialysis centre, a session usually takes about 4 hours, three times a week. Home dialysis may use shorter sessions more often.

What is the difference between haemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis?

Haemodialysis cleans the blood outside the body with a machine and a filter. Peritoneal dialysis uses the lining of the belly as the filter, with fluid put into the belly through a tube, and is often done at home.

How does a dialysis machine work step by step?

The patient is connected through a fistula or catheter. A blood pump moves blood into the dialyser. Inside, waste and extra salt pass through a thin membrane into the dialysate, and the machine removes extra water. Clean blood passes an air detector and returns to the body. Alarms watch pressure, air and leaks the whole time.

What is dialysate made of?

Dialysate is very pure water mixed with measured amounts of salts and minerals such as sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium and bicarbonate, and often a little glucose. The machine mixes it from concentrate and purified RO water, and warms it close to body temperature.

Why do blood and dialysate flow in opposite directions?

Counter-current flow keeps the difference in waste levels between the blood and the fresh dialysate high along the whole filter. That makes waste move out of the blood faster, so cleaning is more efficient.

How does the machine know how much water to remove?

Before each session the patient is weighed. The care team compares this with the patient's target or 'dry' weight and sets the ultrafiltration amount on the machine, which then removes that water at a controlled rate.

Why is a blood thinner used during dialysis?

Blood can clot when it touches tubing and the filter. A blood thinner, usually heparin, is given during the session to keep blood flowing smoothly through the circuit, at a dose set by the care team.
