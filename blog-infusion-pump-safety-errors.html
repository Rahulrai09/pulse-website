Infusion Pump Safety: Common Errors and How to Prevent Them

Infusion and syringe pumps make drug delivery more precise, but programming mistakes and pump faults can still harm patients. This guide explains the common problems reported to the US FDA and the practical steps nurses and hospitals use to prevent them.

How Big Is the Problem?

From 2005 to 2009, the US FDA received about 56,000 adverse event reports linked to infusion pumps, including injuries and deaths, and manufacturers ran 87 recalls. Problems were seen across many brands and pump types. See our how an infusion pump works guide for the basics.

Common Causes of Pump Problems

CauseExample
Software faultsAn alarm that fails to sound, or sounds when nothing is wrong
Confusing screens (user interface)Unclear on-screen steps that lead to a wrong dose
Broken mechanical partsDamaged doors or latches
Battery failuresThe pump stops when moved off mains power

These can lead to too much or too little medicine, or a delay in treatment.

Safety Steps for Nurses

  • Check and verify: confirm the dose, rate and volume at every shift change, after any setting change, when a new bag is hung and when new tubing is primed.
  • Double-check high-risk drugs: have a second nurse independently check settings for drugs such as insulin, heparin, vasopressors, propofol, TPN and morphine.
  • Label: label each pump channel and each line at the point of entry with the drug or fluid name.
  • Monitor: watch the patient for signs of too much or too little, using the monitor, SpO2, EtCO2 or glucose readings where relevant.
  • Follow the 5 rights: right patient, drug, dose, route and time.
  • Take faulty pumps out of use: tag and remove any pump that is damaged, alarms without reason or does not work as expected.

These steps come from the US FDA's risk reduction advice for clinicians. See also our EtCO2 and patient monitor normal values guides.

Have a Back-Up Plan

Every ward should know how to get a working pump and tubing quickly if one fails. For high-risk infusions, a nurse stays with the patient while another fetches a replacement. For patients at risk of fluid overload, clamp and disconnect the line until a new pump is ready.

Steps for Hospital Managers and Biomedical Teams

  • Review incident reports to see where, when and why pump problems happen.
  • Keep instructions and troubleshooting guides easy to reach on every ward.
  • Train "super-users" in each area and offer training on every shift.
  • Have a system to receive and act on recalls and safety alerts.
  • Keep a list of pump software versions and follow the maker's preventive maintenance schedule.

Infusion Pump Safety: FAQs

What are the most common infusion pump errors?

Software faults such as alarm failures, confusing screens that lead to wrong doses, broken mechanical parts and battery failures, according to the US FDA.

How can nurses prevent infusion pump errors?

Verify dose, rate and volume at every change, get a second check for high-risk drugs, label every line, monitor the patient and remove faulty pumps.

Which drugs need a double check on a pump?

High-risk drugs such as insulin, heparin, vasopressors, propofol, TPN and morphine, as set by hospital policy.

What should you do if an infusion pump fails?

Stay with high-risk patients while another nurse gets a working pump. For patients at risk of fluid overload, clamp and disconnect the line first.

How often should infusion pumps be serviced?

Follow the maker's preventive maintenance schedule and keep a record of each pump's software version.
