Dialysis Machine Alarms Explained: What Each Alarm Means

A haemodialysis machine watches the blood and the dialysate all the time and sounds an alarm when something is outside safe limits. This guide explains each common alarm, what the machine does, the usual causes and what staff check first.

PBy Pulse Team · Updated November 17, 2026 · 4 min read

A dialysis machine has two groups of alarms. Blood alarms (arterial and venous pressure, air detector, blood leak) stop the blood pump to protect the patient. Dialysate alarms (conductivity and temperature) send the dialysate to the drain so unsafe fluid never reaches the dialyser.

Two Circuits, Two Kinds of Alarm

A haemodialysis machine has a blood circuit (blood from the patient, through the dialyser and back) and a dialysate circuit (the cleaning fluid the machine mixes and warms). Each has its own monitors.

Alarm groupExamplesWhat the machine does
Blood alarmsArterial pressure, venous pressure, air detector, blood leakStops the blood pump; the air alarm also clamps the venous line
Dialysate alarmsConductivity, temperatureBypasses the dialysate to the drain, away from the dialyser

Arterial Pressure Alarm

The arterial monitor reads the suction between the access and the blood pump. Normally this is negative.

  • Too negative (low): a poorly placed arterial needle, kinked tubing, a drop in the patient's blood pressure or a narrowed access.
  • Too high (less negative): a disconnection between the patient and the pump, an empty saline bag during priming or a pump problem.

Venous Pressure Alarm

  • High: a kinked venous line, a clotted venous chamber, a poorly placed venous needle or a narrowed vein.
  • Low: may mean the venous line has come apart. This is one of the most dangerous alarms, because blood can be pumped out of the body. Keep venous limits tight and check every connection.

Air Detector Alarm

An ultrasonic sensor on the venous line, below the venous chamber, watches for air or foam. If it finds any, the machine stops the blood pump and clamps the venous line so air cannot reach the patient. Common causes are an empty saline bag, a low level in a drip chamber or a leak before the pump. Staff remove the air from the venous chamber and fix the cause before restarting.

Blood Leak Alarm

A light sensor in the used dialysate line checks that the fluid is clear. Blood there usually means a tear in the dialyser membrane, so the pump stops. Air bubbles or a dirty sensor can cause false alarms, but staff should treat it as a real leak until a test strip on the dialysate shows otherwise.

Conductivity and Temperature Alarms

Conductivity shows whether the dialysate has the right salt balance. The wrong mix can upset the patient's blood salts or damage red blood cells. Temperature must also stay in range. If either is out of range, the machine sends the dialysate to the drain. Staff check the concentrate connections and the acid and bicarbonate supply.

Good Practice for Dialysis Units

  • Never silence an alarm without checking the patient and the circuit first.
  • Keep pressure limits set tightly around each patient's normal readings.
  • Tape and check all line connections, especially the venous needle.
  • Use a new transducer protector for every session and replace it if it gets wet.
  • Log alarms and follow the machine maker's service schedule.

Dialysis Machine Alarms: Key Facts

  • Blood alarms (pressure, air, blood leak) stop the blood pump.
  • Dialysate alarms (conductivity, temperature) send the dialysate to the drain.
  • The air detector also clamps the venous line so air cannot reach the patient.
  • A low venous pressure alarm can mean a disconnected line and is one of the most dangerous alarms.
  • Treat a blood leak alarm as real until a dialysate test strip shows otherwise.

Dialysis Machine Alarms: FAQs

What are the main alarms on a dialysis machine?

Arterial pressure, venous pressure, air detector, blood leak, conductivity and temperature alarms.

What does a dialysis machine do when an air alarm sounds?

It stops the blood pump and clamps the venous line so air cannot reach the patient. Staff remove the air and fix the cause before restarting.

What causes a high venous pressure alarm?

A kinked venous line, a clotted venous chamber, a poorly placed venous needle or a narrowed vein.

Why is a low venous pressure alarm dangerous?

It may mean the venous line has come apart, so blood could be pumped out of the body. Keep limits tight and check connections.

What does a blood leak alarm mean?

Blood in the used dialysate, usually from a torn dialyser membrane. Treat it as real until a test strip shows otherwise.
