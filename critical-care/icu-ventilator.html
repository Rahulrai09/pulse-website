How it works

The Pulse ICU Ventilator, Step by Step

From the youngest neonate to an adult with ARDS, here is what the ventilator does at each stage of care.

01

One ventilator for every ICU

Neonate, child and adult on one machine

The Pulse ICU ventilator covers the whole patient range, so a hospital can train staff, stock spares and plan service around one model.

  • Adult: tidal volume 100–4000 ml, breath rate 1–100/min
  • Paediatric: tidal volume 20–300 ml, breath rate 1–100/min
  • Neonate: tidal volume 2–100 ml, breath rate 1–150/min, with a dedicated neonatal flow sensor

02

Invasive ventilation

12 invasive modes for adults

Through a breathing tube, the ventilator offers volume, pressure and dual-control modes: VCV, PCV, SIMV-V, SIMV-P, CPAP/PSV, PRVC, SIMV-PRVC, DualVent, APRV, VS, SMV and CPRV. Paediatric and neonatal patients get the modes suited to them. Apnea backup ventilation takes over if the patient stops breathing.

New to the modes? Read ventilator modes explained.

03

Non-invasive ventilation and oxygen therapy

From mask support to high-flow oxygen

The same machine gives non-invasive ventilation through a mask (CPAP/PSV, PCV, SIMV-P, DualVent, APRV, PSV-S/T; nCPAP for neonates) and high-flow oxygen therapy. This supports sequential therapy: moving a patient from invasive ventilation to mask support and then to oxygen without changing machines.

04

See how the lungs are working

Monitoring, waveforms, loops and lung tools

Beyond pressure, volume and rate, the ventilator measures resistance, compliance, intrinsic PEEP (PEEPi), RSBI, work of breathing, P0.1 and NIF, which help with weaning decisions. Waveforms and loops show pressure, flow, volume and CO2 breath by breath.

Built-in tools include a lung recruitment tool, a pressure–volume (PV) tool and PEEPi monitoring. An optional mainstream CO2 module measures end-tidal CO2 from 0 to 150 mmHg.

05

Alarms, trends and records

Adjustable limits and a 72-hour history

Alarm limits can be set for minute volume, tidal volume, airway pressure, breath rate, FiO2 and apnea time. The ventilator also warns of low battery, low gas supply pressure, airway obstruction, a disconnected tube and high PEEP.

Trends cover the last 72 hours in table or graph form, the event log keeps up to 5,000 alarm and operation entries, and up to 50 screen captures can be saved for review. See ventilator alarms explained.

06

Built for Indian ICUs

Turbine airflow, battery backup, standard ports

A turbine generates the airflow, so the ventilator does not need a compressed air supply; it runs on oxygen from a high-pressure pipeline or cylinder (0.28–0.65 MPa) or a low-pressure source. It takes 100–240 V mains power and holds one or two lithium-ion batteries for power cuts and transfers.

Network, RS-232, nurse call, HDMI and USB ports connect it to hospital systems. Pulse critical care equipment carries CDSCO and CE approval and is made under ISO 13485.

How to Choose an ICU Ventilator

An ICU ventilator breathes for patients who cannot breathe well enough on their own. This is called mechanical ventilation, a form of life support in intensive care: the machine pushes oxygen-rich air into the lungs and lets carbon dioxide out. The Pulse ICU ventilator covers neonates, children and adults on one machine, with invasive ventilation (through a breathing tube), non-invasive ventilation (through a mask) and high-flow oxygen therapy. Using one model across age groups means one set of training, spares and service.

Modes in simple words: in volume control (VCV) the machine gives a set amount of air with each breath; in pressure control (PCV) it pushes air up to a set pressure; SIMV mixes machine breaths with the patient's own; CPAP/PSV supports a patient who is breathing but needs help, and is often used while weaning. What to Check Before Buying an ICU Ventilator

  • Patient range. Confirm neonatal, paediatric and adult use if one model must serve all ICUs.
  • Modes. Match the invasive and non-invasive modes to how your intensivists work.
  • Gas supply. A turbine ventilator like this one makes its own airflow, so it does not need a compressed air supply. It still needs oxygen.
  • Battery backup. Essential for transfers and power cuts.
  • Consumables. Price circuits, filters, flow sensors and masks.
  • Training and service. Ask for on-site training and a clear service response plan.

What We Actually Checked Before Finalizing This Ventilator

A ventilator is not a product you buy on specs alone. Before this one went into our lineup, we looked hard at two things hospitals rarely ask about upfront: how it behaves on unstable mains power, and how fast a service engineer can reach it if something goes wrong. Indian ICUs do not always run on clean, constant power, so battery backup time and the way the unit handles voltage swings matter as much as the ventilation modes it supports.

On the clinical side, we paid attention to alarm behavior under real conditions, not demo conditions. A ventilator that triggers false alarms during routine suction or patient movement trains staff to ignore warnings, which is dangerous. This one is tuned to flag genuine issues like high pressure or disconnection without nagging the team over normal care activity. It supports the standard adult and pediatric modes ICUs expect, with a straightforward interface that does not require a long training cycle for new staff.
