Infusion Pump or Syringe Pump?

Both pumps give medicines and fluids at a set rate, so nurses do not have to count drips. They differ in what they push and how precisely.

PumpDelivers fromRange and accuracyBest for
Infusion PumpIV bag or bottle through a standard infusion set0.1–1500 ml/h, ±5% accuracyFluids, antibiotics, larger volumes
Syringe Pump (this page)Syringe (5–50 ml)0.1–1500 ml/h, ±2% accuracySmall, precise doses: sedation, inotropes, insulin, neonates

Choose the syringe pump for strong medicines given in small, steady amounts, where a small change in rate matters, such as in ICUs and NICUs. Most ICUs use both.

What to Check Before Buying Syringe Pumps

  • Number per bed. ICU beds often need more than one pump; count by bed type.
  • Disposables. Check which infusion sets or syringe brands the pump accepts.
  • Alarms. Occlusion, air-in-line, near-empty and low battery alarms protect patients.
  • Battery life. Matters for patient transfers and power cuts.
  • Mounting. Check pole clamps and stacking if several pumps share one pole.
  • Training and service. Ask for staff training and quick repair support.

Precision Dosing in Small Volumes

Some of the most critical drugs in a hospital are given in tiny volumes: insulin, sedatives, vasoactive medications. At that scale, a small error in rate is a large error in dose. This syringe pump is built around that reality, with occlusion detection sensitive enough to catch a blocked line early and a dose-rate accuracy that holds steady across the full range the ICU and OT actually use, not just the midpoint shown in a spec sheet.

It is also genuinely portable. Its battery is rated to outlast a typical patient transfer, and the compact body fits onto a standard IV pole or bed rail without extra brackets. For a ward or ICU that moves patients between departments several times a day, that portability is not a convenience feature, it is the difference between continuous dosing and a dangerous gap.
