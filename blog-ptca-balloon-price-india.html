What Is a PTCA Balloon?

PTCA stands for percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty: opening a narrowed heart artery from inside, through a small cut in the wrist or groin. A PTCA balloon is a thin tube (catheter) with a small balloon at its tip. The cardiologist slides it over a guidewire to the narrowing and inflates it with an inflation device. The balloon presses the fatty plaque against the wall and widens the artery. It is then deflated and removed.

Most angioplasties use more than one balloon: one to prepare the artery, the balloon that carries the stent, and often a high-pressure balloon to finish the job.

Drug-coated (DCB)Semi-compliant balloon that releases a drug into the wallTreating the artery without leaving a stent

Each balloon has two key pressures. The nominal pressure is where it reaches its labelled size. The rated burst pressure (RBP) is the highest pressure it is tested to safely. A compliance chart shows the size at every pressure in between.

What Is a Drug-Coated Balloon and When Is It Used?

A drug-coated balloon (DCB) is coated with a drug, usually paclitaxel or sirolimus. When it is inflated for 30 to 60 seconds, the drug passes into the artery wall to help stop the artery narrowing again. Nothing is left behind once the balloon is removed.

  • In-stent restenosis. This is the best-supported use. The 2018 ESC/EACTS guidelines gave DCBs a Class IA recommendation for narrowing inside an old stent.
  • Small vessels. Trials such as BASKET-SMALL 2 found DCBs did as well as modern drug-eluting stents in small arteries over 12 months.
  • Large vessels and bifurcations. Evidence is still limited; most new narrowings are still treated with stents.

Good preparation matters. Reviews stress opening the narrowing well first with an SC or NC balloon before using the DCB. There are few direct trials comparing sirolimus and paclitaxel balloons, and reviews warn that results from one brand cannot be assumed for another. The choice between a DCB and a stent is always the treating cardiologist's.

What Changes the Price of a PTCA Balloon?

  • Type. Plain SC and NC balloons cost least; scoring balloons and drug-coated balloons cost much more.
  • Profile. Lower tip and crossing profiles for tight narrowings add cost.
  • Pressure rating. NC balloons with high rated burst pressures and low growth cost more to make.
  • Drug and coating. A DCB's drug, dose and coating technology drive most of its price.
  • Brand and supply terms. Imported brands, consignment stock and the range of sizes kept in the cath lab all change the final price.

What to Check Before Buying PTCA Balloons

  • CDSCO licence and CE or ISO 13485 certificates for each balloon type
  • Nominal and rated burst pressures, and the full compliance chart
  • Tip entry and crossing profiles for tight or calcified lesions
  • Diameters and lengths that match your case mix, including 1.0 to 1.5 mm sizes
  • Rapid exchange (RX) shaft, guiding catheter (usually 5F) and 0.014 inch guidewire compatibility
  • For DCBs: the drug, the dose per mm² and the evidence for that specific balloon
  • Sterile packing, expiry dates and how quickly missing sizes are replaced

PTCA Balloon Price in India: FAQs

How much does a PTCA balloon cost in India?

Plain PTCA balloons (semi-compliant or non-compliant) are listed at about Rs 2,750 to Rs 7,000 in India, and scoring balloons at about Rs 14,000 (TradeIndia, October 2026). Drug-coated balloons cost more; listings we found ranged from about Rs 6,400 to Rs 29,000.

Is the PTCA balloon price capped like stents?

No. The NPPA ceiling price covers coronary stents (Rs 39,186 + GST for a drug-eluting stent). Balloons are not part of that ceiling, so their prices vary by brand, type and supply terms.

What is the difference between SC and NC balloons?

A semi-compliant (SC) balloon grows a little as pressure rises and is used to open a narrowed artery before a stent. A non-compliant (NC) balloon keeps its size at high pressure and is used after a stent to expand it fully.

What is a CTO balloon?

A very small balloon, usually 1.0 to 1.25 mm, used to cross and open a chronic total occlusion (an artery that has been fully blocked for a long time) before larger balloons are used.

What is a drug-coated balloon used for?

A drug-coated balloon delivers a drug such as sirolimus or paclitaxel into the artery wall without leaving a stent. Its best-supported use is in-stent restenosis, where the 2018 ESC/EACTS guidelines gave it a Class IA recommendation. It is also used in small vessels.
